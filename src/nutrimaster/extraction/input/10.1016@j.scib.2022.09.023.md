# Copalyl diphosphate synthase gene TaCPS1 mediated resistance to Fusarium graminearum in wheat in response to Piriformospora indica colonization

Liang Li1 $\textcircled{1}$ · Fuyan Qi1  · Ruiying Hao1

Received: 31 January 2025 / Accepted: 16 November 2025 / Published online: 26 November 2025 $\circledcirc$ The Author(s), under exclusive licence to Springer-Verlag GmbH Germany, part of Springer Nature 2025

# Abstract

Wheat is a vital global crop, essential for food security, but its production is threatened by soil-borne diseases like Fusarium graminearum. This pathogen infects wheat at all growth stages and produces harmful mycotoxins, severely compromising both yield and quality. Piriformospora indica, endophytic fungi of plant rhizosphere, can not only efectively promote growth and development, but also improve disease resistance in plants. Here, the mechanism of TaCPS1 gene expression induced by $P .$ indica colonization to improve wheat resistance to $F .$ graminearum has been investigated. Results showed that colonization of P. indica decreased infection of $F .$ graminearum and correspondingly reduced deoxynivalenol (DON) content in wheat roots. Transcriptome sequence analysis demonstrated colonization of $P .$ indica leads to a high expression of the synthase gene family of diterpenoid metabolites. TaCPS1 gene, a crucial gene in the synthesis of diterpenoid metabolites, responded promptly to the colonization of $P .$ . indica. Subsequently, 31 transcription factors were screened by yeast one-hybrid library screening and TaZF-HD5 was verifed to be interacted with the promoter of TaCPS1. The fndings indicate that plants overexpression TaCPS1 demonstrated heightened responsiveness to P. indica colonization and exhibited enhanced resistance to root rot. The increased phytocassane content in TaCPS1 overexpression lines implies that TaCPS1 plays positive role in phytoalexin biosynthesis. The upregulated expression of TaCPS1 in response to the colonization of $P .$ . indica, thereby increasing the content of phytocassane synthesis, is one of the reasons why P. indica mediates wheat disease resistance to $F .$ . graminearum.

# Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum) is one of the three major cereal crops, and its productivity and quality are directly related to food security and social stability. However, various biotic and abiotic stresses pose serious threats to wheat yield, particularly fungal infections. Fusarium graminearum (F. graminearum) causes rot in roots, stems and heads, resulting in signifcant yield losses. Furthermore, mycotoxins produced during the infection process, such as deoxynivalenol (DON), zearalenone (ZEN) and nivalenol (NIV), which signifcantly afect the health of human beings and livestock, reduce grain quality (Armer et al. 2024). Nevertheless, the

overuse of agrochemicals and fungicides can lead to pathogen resistance and adverse environmental impacts (Fenta et al. 2024). Therefore, with increase in environmental protection awareness, biocontrol is gradually being recognized as an alternative strategy to chemical control (Ahmad et al. 2023). The use of biocontrol microorganism, which induce resistance against pathogens, as a more environmentally friendly and efcient biocontrol measure, has been the subject of extensive research (Hussain et al. 2024).

Piriformospora indica (P. indica), a root endophytic fungus, was isolated in 1998 from the Thar Desert in northwestern India. It exhibits a broad host range, encompassing both monocots and dicots. In addition to promoting plant biomass and nutrient uptake, P. indica has been demonstrated to confer plant resistance to pathogens including Fusarium (Cheng et al. 2022; Ghezel Sefoo et al. 2019; Wu et al. 2022), Rhizoctonia (Kheyri et al. 2022) and Zymoseptoria tritici (Ashraf et al. 2021) through activating oxidative systems, inducing defense-related gene expression and stimulating hormonal metabolic pathways. Researchers

have further extensively studied the molecular mechanisms underlying $P .$ indica-mediated disease resistance. P. indica colonization in Lobelia roots could modulate the expression of nucleotide binding site-leucine rich repeat (NBS-LRR) Resistance (R) to confer protection against Eubacterium chrysanthemum infection (Ye et al. 2019). P. indica enhanced resistance to onion leaf blight through the regulation of defense-related genes expression, including LOX, PAL, GST and WRKY (Roylawar et al. 2021). Similarly, our previous study revealed that wheat root colonization with $P .$ . indica modifed mRNA transcriptome alterations induced by $F .$ . graminearum, leading to an increased expression of resistance-related genes and a subsequent mitigation of root rot disease (Li et al. 2022). Notably, P. indica colonization can alter the metabolic pathways of plant diterpenoids.

Diterpenoids are a large number of structurally diverse secondary metabolites in plant. In addition to gibberellins, which stimulate plant growth and development, diterpenoid metabolites contain phytoalexins that confer resistance to pathogens and play a crucial role in plant responses to biotic stress (Yi et al. 2019). Momilactones, phytocassanes, oryzalexins and ent-10-oxodepressin have been isolated from rice based on their accumulation in response to elicitation and their antibiotic activity against M. oryzae (Kato-Noguchi and Peters. 2013; Schmelz et al. 2014). The identifcation of Rhizopus microsporus-induced metabolites in maize led to the discovery of the diterpenoid phytoalexins kauralexins A3 and B3 (Schmelz et al. 2011). However, diterpenoid phytoalexins have yet to be identifed in monocotyledons beyond rice and maize (Murphy et al. 2020).

The Class II diterpene synthase copalyl diphosphate synthase (CPS) and the Class I diterpene synthase kaurene synthase (KS) are pivotal enzymes in diterpenoids biosynthesis. (E, E, E)-geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate (GGDP), produced via the MEP pathway, is catalyzed by type II diterpene cyclase CPS. This enzyme facilitates a protonation-induced cycloaddition reaction that selectively yields cyclic hydrocarbon structures with defned stereoconformations (Köksal et al. 2011). Subsequently, these products undergo modifcation or rearrangement by a type I diterpene cyclase, leading to the formation of a basic cyclic skeleton.

The classifcation and functions of these enzyme classes are well characterized in rice, with three CPSs and six KSLs identifed (Otomo et al. 2004; Prisic et al. 2004). By exploring the preliminary studies about the CPS and KSL gene families in wheat, some function-known CPSs and KSLs were concluded in our work (Fig. S1). Among these, TaCPS1 and TaCPS3 catalyzed the production of ent-CDP, while TaCPS2 solely catalyzes the production of normal CDP, and TaCPS4 catalyzes the formation of both ent-CDP and normal CDP (Wu et al. 2012). Similarly, wheat possesses a diverse array of KSLs that react with ent-CDP, a common intermediate in gibberellin biosynthesis, and

normal CDP (Zhou et al. 2012). However, the end products of these metabolic processes and their functions need to be further investigated. It has been demonstrated that the transcription of OsCPS2 and OsCPS4 can be induced in rice by chitin, methyl jasmonate or UV irradiation (Toyomasu et al. 2015), and similarly, TaCPS1 and TaCPS2 in wheat also exhibited inducible transcription.

Here, we investigated the efects of $P .$ . indica inoculation on wheat growth and resistance to $F .$ . graminearum, and further elucidated the underlying mechanisms. In particular, we characterized the role of the TaCPS gene in the defense response against $F .$ graminearum infection under P. indica colonization in wheat roots. By revealing the interaction between TaCPS and $P .$ indica during root colonization, this study provides new insights into the mechanisms of fungus-mediated disease resistance in plants. Although the study focuses on the molecular interplay between P. indica and wheat, the conservation of the diterpenoid biosynthesis pathway across monocots and dicots implies that the uncovered CPS-dependent mechanisms may also extend to other cereal crops. Nevertheless, further validation in other agronomically important species is needed to confrm the broader applicability of these fndings.

# Materials and methods

# Plant materials and growth condition

Piriformospora indica DSM 11827 was selected for this study due to its well-established role in promoting plant growth and inducing broad-spectrum resistance in a variety of host plants, as extensively documented in previous literature. P. indica was provided by Karl-Heinz Kogel at the IPAZ, Giessen University, Germany. F. graminearum and the wheat seeds were provided by Tianjin Academy of Agricultural Sciences, China. Four-week-old P. indica plates were used to collect spore suspension using sterile $0 . 0 5 \%$ Tween-20. Subsequently, the fltrate was diluted to a concentration of $1 \times 1 0 ^ { 5 }$ chlamydospores/mL. Wheat seeds were surface-disinfected with $7 0 \%$ ethanol and $1 5 \%$ sodium hypochlorite and rinsed fve times with sterilized water. The seeds were then immersed in the chlamydospore suspensions for 2 days, with $0 . 0 5 \%$ Tween-20 solution as mock. The germinated seeds were further transferred to pots $( 1 5 \times 1 5 ~ \mathrm { c m } )$ ) flled with a 1:1 mixture of sterilized sand and soil, and incubated in a greenhouse under a $1 6 \mathrm { h }$ light and 8 h dark cycle at $2 3 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . After 7 days, toothpick fragments laden with $F .$ . graminearum hypha were inserted into the stem of each wheat plant.

# DON production assay

DON production in roots was quantifed using a DON ELISA Kit. Briefy, the substrate TMB was converted to a blue product by catalysis of peroxidase and subsequently turned yellow upon acid addition. Fifty $\mu \mathrm { L }$ samples, reference standards, and HRP-labeled competing antigens were added to microtiter wells with DON antigens. The micro-well plate was then incubated at $3 7 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for $6 0 ~ \mathrm { { m i n } }$ and washed fve times. Fifty $\mu \mathrm { L }$ substrate was added to each well, followed by mixing with $5 0 ~ \mu \mathrm { L }$ stopping solution after a 15-min incubation in dark. The OD value was detected on ELISA at $4 5 0 ~ \mathrm { n m }$ to calculate sample concentration.

# RNA extraction, mRNA library construction and sequencing

For RNA-seq analysis, three independent biological replicates were used per condition (Mock vs. P. indica-colonized). Each replicate consisted of pooled root tissue from 15 plants. For pathogen challenge assays, a minimum of 12 plants per treatment were used, and the entire experiment was repeated three times independently $\mathrm { { \acute { n } } } = 3$ biological replicates). Total RNA was extracted from the wheat roots of each treatment 14 days post-infection using Trizol reagent. RNA integrity was confrmed using a $1 \%$ agarose gel. The mRNA was purifed using OligodT-attached-magnetic beads and fragmented with fragmentation bufer, which were used to synthesize the frst-strand cDNA with random hexamer primers, followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis. ER/A-tailing and adapter ligation were added to terminal repair. Then, the cDNA fragments underwent PCR amplifcation and were purifed using Ampure XP Beads. The heated and denatured PCR products were cyclized by a bridge primer to construct the cDNA library. The resulting cDNA libraries were then sequenced on the BGIseq500 platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China).

# Bioinformatic analysis of transcriptome data

Clean RNA-seq reads were realigned to the wheat genome using hierarchical indexing for spliced alignment of transcripts (HISAT). String Tie was employed to quantify expression levels. Diferentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by an absolute $\log _ { 2 } \mathrm { F C }$ value $> 1$ and $p$ -value $< 0 . 0 5$ . Subsequent annotation of DEGs utilized the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (http://www.genome.jp/kegg) database. Expression heatmaps were generated using the Pheatmap R package.

# Quantitative real‑time PCR (qRT‑PCR) analysis

Total RNA was isolated using Trizol according to the manufacturer’s protocol. First-strand cDNA synthesized using M-MLV reserve transcriptase was used as template. The qRT-PCR was performed with SYBR Green Master Mix on Roche LightCycler 96. For each reaction solution, $1 0 \mu \mathrm { L }$ of $2 \times$ UltraSYBR Mixture, $1 \mu \mathrm { L }$ of forward primer and reverse primer, $2 ~ { \mu \mathrm { g } }$ of cDNA template and $\mathrm { d d H } _ { 2 } \mathrm { O }$ were added to a fnal volume of $2 0 \mu \mathrm { L }$ . The amplifcation program was as follows: initial denaturation at $9 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles, each consisting of denaturation, annealing and extension at $9 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for 30 s, $5 7 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for 30 s and $7 2 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ for 32 s, respectively. The PCR results were analyzed using the comparative $2 ^ { - \Delta \Delta \mathrm { C T } }$ method (Livak et al. 2001). TaActin was applied as the reference gene. Gene-specific primers, designed from Primer 3, are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

# Quantifcation of fungal biomass

Genomic DNA was extracted form wheat roots with HP Plant DNA Kit following the manufacturer’s instruction. The fungal biomass was evaluated by qRT-PCR using specifc primers of $F .$ graminearum $\beta$ -tublin and ITS for $P .$ . indica (Table S1). Moreover, specifc primers of wheat Actin gene (Table S1) were used to measure the plant DNA. Operation was the same as previous. Relative amounts of fungal DNA were calculated via 2−ΔΔCT a $2 ^ { - \Delta \Delta \mathrm { C T } }$ nd normalized to the TaActin.

# Construction and analysis of phylogenetic tree, conserved motifs and cis‑acting regulatory elements (CAREs)

TaCPS, OsCPS and ZmCPS were identifed and aligned using ClustalW and the phylogenetic tree was constructed based on neighbor-joining (NJ) method with 1000 bootstrap by MEGA11 software. The phylogenetic tree was normalized using iTOL (https://itol.embl.de). Ten conserved motifs were identifed by using MEME (Multiple Expectation Maximization for Motif Elication) (https://meme-suite. org/meme/tools/meme) and visualized by TBtools. $2 0 0 0 \mathrm { k b }$ upstream sequences of each gene were extracted with Fasta Extract Representative of TBtools. Cis-acting regulatory elements were further predicted using PlantCare database (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/ html/) and the distribution of CAREs was visualized by TBtools.

# Chromosome distribution and synteny analysis

The gene replication events were analyzed using multiple collinear scanning toolkits (MCScanX). DNA Sequence

Polymorphism (DnaSP) was used to calculate the ratio of nonsynonymous substitution rate (Ka) and synonymous substitution rate (Ks) of duplication gene pairs. $\mathrm { K a } / \mathrm { K s } > 1$ represents gene experienced position selection, $\mathrm { K a } / \mathrm { K s } = 1$ means neutral selection, and $\mathrm { K a / K s } < 1$ signifes negative purify selection. The homology and synteny relationship were exhibited by Advanced Circos and Multiple Synteny Plot of TBtools. Meanwhile, the chromosomal physical location was marked on the basis of genome annotation information.

# Yeast one‑hybrid (Y1H) library screening

The 510 bp upstream region of TaCPS1 was cloned into the pHIS2 vector digested with SacI and KpnI. Then, the bait plasmid of pHIS2 was transformed into the yeast strain Y187. Enriched cDNA was ligated into the pminHIS3 vector. The plasmids of the cDNA library were transformed into competent cells of the bait strain. The transformed yeast cells were further cultured on the plates of SD/-TLH with 75 mM 3AT for 3–7 d at $3 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ to isolate the positive yeast colonies. The single colonies were collected using $2 \times \mathrm { Y P D A }$ solution and performed by PCR. The PCR products were identifed by NGS analysis.

To confrm the candidate, transcription factors (TFs) screened can bind to the promoter fragment of TaCPS1, the full-length CDS of LOC123074562 was recombined into pGADT7. The prey plasmid was further transformed into Y187 strain which previously has been transformed with bait plasmids pHIS2. The positive and negative controls were conducted under the same condition.

# Dual‑luciferase reporter assay

Dual-luciferase reporter assay was always performed to confrm the interaction between transcription factors and the promoter in plants. The promoter fragment of TaCPS1 was inserted into pGreenII 0800-LUC with HindIII and XhoI as reporter plasmid. The sequence of LOC123074562 was fused into pGreenII 62-SK plasmid as efector. Then reporter and efector were transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 (pSoup), respectively. The two transformants were mixed 1:1 and injected into leaves of N. benthamiana to infect. Following a period of one day in the dark and 2 days in the light, the frefy luciferase substrate from the dual-luciferase reporter kit (Beyotime, Beijing, China) was applied to the abaxial surface of the leaves. The resulting luminescence was then observed using IVIS Lumina III (PerkinElmer, USA). The ratio of LUC (Firefy luciferase) and REN (Renilla luciferase) activity, a position control, was determined with dual-luciferase reporter assay kit (Beyotime, Beijing, China).

The JASPAR website (https://jaspar.elixir.no/) was utilized to predict the binding sites of the transcription factor

ZF-HD5 to the TaCPS1 gene. Based on the predicted binding site sequences, six base pair segments were randomly modified to synthesize single strand of complementary DNA. The primers of TaCPS1-mut-F and TaCPS1-mut-R (Table1) were used to amplify TaCPS1-pro mutant fragments via PCR. To further verify the impact of these mutations on binding ability of ZF-HD5, the synthesized TaCPS1-pro-Mut fragments were cloned into the vector pGreenⅡ 0800-por-nLUC. Subsequently, these reporter gene vectors with the ZF-HD5 expression vector were cotransfected into tobacco leaf cells, and luciferase activity was measured to determine whether the mutations will afect interaction between ZF-HD5 on TaCPS1 gene.

# Transformation vectors and generation of transgenic wheat

For silencing of wheat TaCPS1, a 352-bp sequence was cloned into the pGEMT vector (http:// takara.company. lookchem.cn/). The partial TaCPS1 segment was then inserted into the destination vector pFGC5941–35S. For overexpression of wheat TaCPS1, a 2394-bp CDS sequence was cloned into pGEMT vector (http:// takara.company. lookchem.cn/). The whole gene of TaCPS1 fragment with 2394 bp was then inserted into the destination VecBar2 binary vector. These constructs were introduced into wheat (cv. Fielder) immature embryos using Agrobacteriummediated transformation as described by Ishida et al. (2015). The empty vector was used as control. The transgenic plants expressing TaCPS1 were verifed by the QPCR.

The gene expression patter was same for those 12 genes and they were all upregulated in P. indica colonization treatment and P. indica $+ F .$ . graminearum infection group. Through gene sequence blast analysis, we identifed TraesCS2B02G445500 as encoding a critical synthetase, known as TaCPS1, involved in diterpenoid biosynthesis. Its expression was signifcantly higher under the $\mathrm { P I } + \mathrm { F G }$ treatment than under the FG treatment alone, showing a 13-fold induction (Fig. 4B), which prompted further investigation into its function. TaCPS1 was annotated as an ent-copalyl diphosphate synthase (CPS), a key rate-limiting enzyme at the gateway to both gibberellin (GA) and phytoalexin biosynthesis pathways. Due to its central regulatory role, TaCPS1 emerged as a high-value candidate for mediating the observed systemic resistance. It exhibited one of the most pronounced and consistent fold change increases in expression upon colonization by P. indica, indicating a robust response to the interaction. Since induced resistance often entails metabolic reprogramming, we hypothesized that P. indica may reconfgure diterpenoid metabolism governed by CPS to enhance

defense priming. TaCPS1 was therefore selected as a key candidate to test this hypothesis.

# Analysis of chromosome distribution and synteny

Chromosome distribution showed that TaCPS genes were predominantly located on chromosome 1, 2 and 7 and evenly distributed in the A, B and D subgenomes (Fig.  S3A). Twenty-one pairs of paralogs gene were identified by MCSanX among 25 TaCPS genes, of which the most gene duplication events occurring on chromosomes 2A and 2D. There were 11 genes undergoing segmental duplication and two groups of tandem duplication events (TraesC-S2A02G027100 and TraesCS2A02G027200, TraesC-S7A02G552600 and TraesCS7A02G552700) tandem duplication events, which suggested that tandem duplication was not the primary mode of TaCPS gene expansion (Fig. S3B). Moreover, Ka/Ks of the 21 paralogs gene pairs was calculated so as to determine whether selective pressures acted on the evolutionary process. It was found that Ka/Ks of all paralog gene pairs was less than 1, which represented negative purify selection under functional constraints. These revealed that duplication of TaCPS relied principally on segmental duplication and purify selection. In order to explore the evolutionary process of TaCPS with other species, we analyzed the orthologs pair genes among the genomes of Triticum aestivum, Oryza sativa and Zea mays L with MCSanX. The synteny analysis presented that there were two TaCPS genes in lining with CPS genes in Oryza sativa, while no genes in Zea mays L (Fig. S3C).

# Discussion

# Overview of the study and key fndings

This study investigated the mechanism by which the root endophytic fungus $P .$ indica enhances wheat resistance to $F .$ graminearum, a major soil-borne pathogen. Colonization of wheat roots by $P .$ . indica signifcantly reduced infection by $F .$ . graminearum and decreased the accumulation of the harmful mycotoxin deoxynivalenol (DON). Transcriptome analysis revealed that P. indica colonization strongly upregulated the diterpenoid metabolite synthase gene family. Among these, the TaCPS1 gene, a key enzyme in the diterpenoid phytoalexin biosynthesis pathway, was rapidly and highly induced. A yeast one-hybrid screen identifed the transcription factor TaZF-HD5, which was experimentally confrmed to bind

directly to the promoter of the TaCPS1 gene, suggesting that it regulates its expression. Wheat lines overexpressing TaCPS1 showed enhanced responsiveness to $P .$ indica and increased resistance to Fusarium root rot. These lines also accumulated higher levels of phytocassane-type diterpenoid phytoalexins. The study concludes that P. indica-induced upregulation of TaCPS1 (potentially via TaZF-HD5) and the consequent increased synthesis of antifungal phytocassanes are a crucial mechanism behind the enhanced resistance to F. graminearum mediated by this benefcial fungus.

# Biocontrol potential of P. indica against F. graminearum in wheat

F. graminearum infestation poses a serious threat to both wheat safety and productivity (Cai et al. 2024). This pathogen is particularly notorious for causing Fusarium head blight, a devastating preharvest disease in cereals (Johns et al. 2022). Furthermore, it constitutes a signifcant component of the fungal community in stored grains, thereby presenting a considerable health risk to consumers (Wang et al. 2024). However, the excessive use of chemical pesticides has led to an increase in the resistance of $F .$ . graminearum. As a result, it is imperative to utilize dependable and efcient biological control methods. P. indica has been widely proved with a signifcant growth-promoting efect, and it has also shown the ability to strength resistance against pathogen in multiple studies (Yan et al. 2021; Jahandideh Mahjen Abadi et al. 2021). However, the role of $P .$ . indica in the molecular regulatory network of plant disease resistance remains unclear. In our research, we investigated the mechanism of TaCPS gene expression induced by $P .$ indica colonization

to improve wheat resistance to $F .$ graminearum. The results indicated that pre-colonization of $P .$ . indica could rescue biomass loss caused by $F .$ . graminearum and decreased the severity of lesions in root and stem of wheat. These results are consistent with previous studies that $P .$ indica could improve resistance of plants to $F .$ . graminearum (Rabiey et al. 2015).

On the basis of the physiological results, the biomass of $F .$ graminearum was quantifed in the wheat roots colonized with and without $P .$ indica. The results demonstrated that pre-colonization of $P .$ . indica reduced the amount of $F .$ . graminearum infection, resulting decreased disease index, which in turn contributed to a corresponding reduction of DON production (Fig. 1). As a protein synthesis inhibitor, excessive ingestion of DON can lead to impaired function of the digestive system and other organs in humans and animals, and may even cause death (Tang et al. 2019). Our result indicated that pre-colonization of P. indica reduced DON production and subsequently, improved wheat quality, which contribute to food security and reduce economic losses in the cereal-based food and feed industries.

# P. indica reprograms diterpenoid biosynthesis via TaCPS1 induction to enhance fusarium resistance in wheat

Analysis of transcription showed that the presence of $P .$ . indica reprogrammed the expression profle of wheat and induced differential gene expression (Fig.  2A). Functional annotation of the DEGs revealed that 25 DEGs were enriched on the diterpenoid synthesis pathway in $\mathrm { P I } + \mathrm { F G }$ vs. FG and 2 DEGs were enriched in PI vs. CK (Fig. 3). As plants attacked by pathogens and pests, diterpenoid phytoalexins are induced to be synthesized and accumulated to restrain pathogen infestation and insect feeding (Smith et al. 1996). It was reported that maize phytoalexins, kauralexins A3 and B3, are induced by Fusarium and are involved in the defense response. It has also been found that certain concentrations of kauralexins have signifcant antifungal activity (Mafu et al. 2018). Similarly, pathogen infestation, UV radiation, jasmonic acid and $\mathrm { C u C l } _ { 2 }$ treatments all induced phytoalexin production, thereby enhancing resistance of plant to biotic and abiotic stress (Toyomasu et al. 2015; Horie et al. 2015). Momilactones A, B and phytocassanes A–E are also secreted outside the root to defend against pathogens (Xu et al. 2012; Toyomasu et al. 2008).

Fungal-induced immune responses can lead to increased activity of diterpene synthetase in plants (Saldivar et al. 2023), including CPS which is a vital enzyme in the synthesis of diterpenoids and gibberellin. The expression of $Z { \bmod { n } } 2$ encoding ent-CPS protein was induced by a Fusarium infestation in maize (Harris et al. 2005). This induction probably bolstered resistance against pathogen by increasing phytoalexin

production (Li et al. 2016). Our results showed that $P .$ . indica pre-colonization signifcantly enhanced TaCPS1 expression and further improved wheat resistance to $F .$ . graminearum (Fig. 5).

The phylogenetic analysis results revealed that TaCPS1 (TraesCS2B02G445500) and TaCPS2 (TraesC-S2A02G027800) were clustered on the same branch with OsCPS2 (OsQ6Z5I0) and OsCPS4 (OsQ0JF02), respectively. Consequently, it can be inferred that TaCPS1 and TaCPS2 are homologs of OsCPS2 and OsCPS4 (Fig. S2A), which was consistent with previous research (Toyomasu et al. 2009). The evolution of TaCPS1 was discussed via synteny analysis and the value of Ka/Ks. The results indicated that the duplication of TaCPS1 relied on segmental duplication and purify selection (Fig. S3).

Toyomasu et al (2009) amplifed three CPS bases (TaCPS1, TaCPS2 and TaCPS3) from wheat according to the expression sequence tag, and expressed them in prokaryotic form of Escherichia coli, proving that the proteins encoded by these three genes had the activity of kobayl pyrophosphate synthase. According to evolutionary tree and expression analysis, TaCPS3 is responsible for the biosynthesis of gibberellin, while TaCPS1 and TaCPS2 may be functionally homologous to OsCPS2 and OsCPS4 in rice and participate in the biosynthesis of plant protection (Toyomasu et al. 2008). In rice (Oryza sativa), the CPS ortholog OsCPS4 is essential for synthesizing diterpenoid phytoalexins (e.g., momilactones) (Niu et al. 2017), which are crucial for defending against blast fungus (Magnaporthe oryzae). Mutants in OsCPS4 are phytoalexin-defcient and highly susceptible (Toyomasu et al. 2014). In Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana), AtCPS is involved in the biosynthesis of diterpenes that contribute to non-host resistance (Fleet et al. 2003). In barley (Hordeum vulgare), HvCPS1 is responsible for synthesizing ent-kaurene, a precursor for GAs, but also for antifungal diterpenoids (Bergman et al. 2024). Our study now provides direct functional evidence that this conserved role of CPS in diterpenoid-based defense is also operational in wheat and is a key mechanism underpinning $P .$ . indica-induced resistance. Our data experimentally verifed that TaCPS1 is likely to participate in the synthesis of plant phytoalexin (phytoassane) (Fig. 7B), which provided further experimental basis for revealing the function of TaCPS1. On the contrary, it also demonstrated that TaCPS1 facilitated the synthesis of phytoalexin content in response to the colonization of P. indica, thereby enhancing the resistance of wheat to $F .$ . graminearum.

# Future perspectives

Based on the fndings of this study, several promising directions for future research can be proposed: 1) Elucidation of the complete signaling pathway: Future work should focus on delineating the complete signaling cascade from P. indica perception to TaZF-HD5 activation and subsequent TaCPS1 induction. Identifying receptors or early signaling components involved in recognizing $P .$ indica will be crucial. 2) Functional characterization of TaZF-HD5: Further investigation into the regulation and function of TaZF-HD5 is needed. This includes examining its expression patterns under various stresses, identifying co-regulators or partners, and validating its role in transgenic wheat plants. 3) Comprehensive analysis of diterpenoid phytoalexins: The specifc diterpenoid phytoalexins (e.g., phytocassanes) conferring resistance should be identifed and quantifed. Their direct antifungal activity against $F .$ graminearum and potential role in reducing DON synthesis warrant detailed metabolic and functional studies. 4) Field evaluation and breeding applications: The efcacy of TaCPS1 overexpression lines should be tested under feld conditions to assess their practical agronomic value. Developing markers for TaCPS1 or its regulators could facilitate marker-assisted breeding for Fusarium resistance.

The feld evaluation and breeding applications are twofold. First, P. indica itself emerges as a promising candidate for development into a novel biocontrol inoculant. Its demonstrated efcacy in enhancing wheat resistance against a major pathogen supports its potential application within integrated pest management strategies. Second, the identifcation of TaCPS1 as a key gene creates opportunities for molecular breeding and biotechnological approaches to engineer wheat varieties with primed defense mechanisms. The commercialization timeline for P. indica-based products will depend on overcoming challenges related to large-scale fermentation, formulation stability, and extensive feld trials—a process that may take approximately fve to eight years. For farmers, adopting such biocontrol agents could yield considerable economic benefts by reducing dependence on chemical pesticides, lowering input costs and minimizing environmental impact, all while maintaining yield stability through strengthened innate resistance.

# Conclusion

Based on the current results, the colonization of P. indica alleviated root rot symptoms caused by $F .$ . graminearum and reduced pathogen colonization in wheat roots, thereby decreasing the level of deoxynivalenol (DON) produced by F. graminearum. RNA-seq analysis revealed that $P .$ indica colonization modulates the diterpene biosynthesis pathway and signifcantly upregulates the expression of TaCPS1, a key enzyme involved in diterpene synthesis. Through yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) screening, a transcription factor, ZF-HD5, induced by P. indica, was identifed to interact with the promoter region of TaCPS1. Given that OsCPS4, a homolog of TaCPS1 in rice, is known to participate in the synthesis of both phytoalexins and gibberellins, it is plausible that P. indica infuences TaCPS1 regulation via induction of ZF-HD5. This interaction may alter phytoalexin production and contribute to enhanced pathogen resistance in wheat. In conclusion, this study provides valuable insights into the potential of leveraging P. indica-mediated diterpenoid biosynthesis to develop wheat varieties with improved resistance to Fusarium diseases, thereby reducing dependence on chemical fungicides and promoting food security.
